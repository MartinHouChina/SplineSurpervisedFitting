\title{Learning-Guided Candidate Knot Selection with KeepMask-Conditioned
Refinement for Compact B-Spline Fitting}
\author{Author names and affiliations to be supplied}
\begin{abstract}
The number and placement of knots strongly affect the accuracy and complexity
of B-spline curve approximation. Jointly determining a discrete knot subset,
continuous knot coordinates, and data parameters is nevertheless a nonlinear
mixed discrete--continuous problem; predicting retention and relocation
independently tends to produce redundant knots or localized error peaks. We
present a learning-guided candidate-selection method for ordered samples. An
order-aware encoder predicts monotone point parameters and redundant ordered
knot candidates. An adaptive KeepMask then selects a variable-size subset, and
the surviving subset conditions the joint correction of point parameters and
knot positions. Control points are obtained by one endpoint-constrained cubic
B-spline least-squares solve rather than network regression. Mean squared
error and maximum pointwise squared error jointly define feasibility, after
which the method seeks fewer internal knots. Repeated training, cross-source,
and multi-tolerance experiments show that the proposed system maintains a
competitive joint-feasibility rate and achieves the lowest overall mean
internal-knot count among the six evaluated implementations at the principal
setting. These results support coupled proposal, selection, and relocation as
an effective accuracy--compactness strategy on the tested data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Approximating ordered samples by B-spline curves is a basic operation in
reverse engineering, trajectory processing, map generalization, and
manufacturing geometry. B-splines provide local control and a compact,
CAD-compatible representation, but their quality is governed by more than the
control vertices alone \cite{piegl1997}. Data parameters determine where the
observations are compared with the curve; the knot vector determines the
support and flexibility of the basis; and the control vertices determine the
embedded geometry. Once the parameters and knots are fixed, the control-point
problem is linear. Optimizing the knot number and knot coordinates as well
turns the task into a nonlinear mixed discrete--continuous problem
\cite{jupp1978,yeh2020}.

The engineering objective is therefore not simply to minimize a residual with
a prescribed dense knot vector. It is to obtain a representation that is both
accurate and economical. Too many knots increase storage, downstream editing
cost, and sensitivity to noise; too few knots remove the local degrees of
freedom needed around geometric features. CAD studies have addressed this
balance using smoothed curvature \cite{li2005}, dominant-point refinement
\cite{park2007}, sparse optimization \cite{kang2015}, and derivative-based
feature distributions \cite{yeh2020}. CAGD work has also learned knot-quality
scores from differential-geometric descriptors \cite{laube2018svm}. These
methods establish the importance of feature-aware knot placement, but the
number of retained knots, their positions, and the data parameterization are
still commonly determined in separate stages or through repeated fitting.

The same coupling appears in surface reconstruction. Feature-sensitive
surface parameterization can allocate more parameter-space resolution to
high-curvature regions even with uniform knots \cite{lai2006}; iterative
geometric fitting updates B-spline surface control meshes from point data
\cite{kineri2012}; and normal-aware objectives improve inter-patch smoothness
\cite{seiler2018}. These surface methods are outside the scope of the present
curve model, but they reinforce a general geometric-design principle:
parameterization, representation capacity, and fitting variables should be
considered together rather than optimized as unrelated outputs.

Learning can amortize this coupled decision over a collection of curves while
retaining a standard B-spline as the deployed representation. The unresolved
difficulty is variable model order. Independently thresholding a fixed set of
candidate scores does not account for interactions among surviving knots: when
one candidate is removed, the neighbouring interval lengths change and the
remaining knots and point parameters may need to move. This observation
motivates a subset-conditioned formulation rather than a terminal binary
pruning layer.

This paper presents a learning-guided candidate-selection pipeline for ordered
curve samples. The network first predicts monotone point parameters and an
overcomplete ordered candidate set. It then constructs one adaptive KeepMask
and uses the selected subset itself to condition survivor interactions,
parameter correction, and bounded knot relocation. The resulting parameters
and knots are passed to a conventional endpoint-constrained least-squares
solver; the network does not regress the control vertices. A separately
reported numerical wrapper is used only in threshold-based evaluation and is
not part of the learned architecture.

The experimental study includes repeated training and validation, four-level
tolerance sweeps, cross-source paired comparisons, and representative case
analyses on held-out synthetic splines, handwriting trajectories, coastlines,
terrain contours, and procedural industrial offsets. Every normalized MSE
target is paired with a maximum pointwise squared-error bound. All attempted
curves remain in the pass-rate denominator, and the study reports feasibility,
internal-knot count, mean and maximum error, and complete runtime. The fixed
paired audit subset used by the main table is specified in Section~7. The five
literature baselines are disclosed repository adaptations; their results should
not be interpreted as exact reproductions of the authors' software.

The contributions of this study are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item An order-aware geometric encoding and local cross-attention proposal
mechanism that combines chord-normalized derivatives, learned point
parameters, and parameter-domain locality to generate ordered, high-recall
candidate knots.
\item A KeepMask-conditioned survivor interaction mechanism in which the hard
subset determines the attention memory, retained-neighbour geometry, updated
point parameters, and relocated knot positions before B-spline refitting.
\item A multi-threshold experimental protocol that reports both MSE and
maximum pointwise squared error, knot complexity, full end-to-end runtime, and
all attempted-curve failures across synthetic, handwriting, geographic, and
procedural CAD-style curves.
\item A cross-source and multi-tolerance study showing that, when feasibility
is judged jointly by MSE and maximum pointwise squared error, the complete
pipeline attains the lowest overall mean internal-knot count among the six
evaluated implementations at the principal setting, while retaining a
competitive joint-feasibility rate.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews
parameterization, knot adaptation, and learning-based spline fitting.
Section~3 defines the sampled error-constrained objective. Sections~4 and~5
describe the complete pipeline and its network components. Section~6 presents
the training objectives, Sections~7 and~8 report the experimental protocol and
results, and Sections~9 and~10 discuss limitations and conclusions.

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Classical parameterization, fitting, and knot adaptation}
For fixed point parameters and a fixed knot vector, B-spline control points
enter the approximation linearly and can be recovered by least squares
\cite{piegl1997,yeh2020}. Allowing knot coordinates to vary produces a
nonlinear free-knot problem even after separating the linear control variables
\cite{jupp1978}. This observation motivates the division used here: the
network predicts parameterization and knot structure, whereas an explicit
linear solver retains responsibility for the control points.

Parameterization establishes the geometric correspondence used by that solve.
Uniform, chord-length, and centripetal rules are simple and stable, but can
produce markedly different residuals \cite{lee1989}; geometric-distance
methods improve the correspondence through iterative updates \cite{wang2006}.
We retain chord length as an anchor rather than a final answer, predict a
bounded monotone correction, and apply the same parameter warp to the knots.
This preserves the robustness of a classical initialization while preventing
samples and knots from drifting into inconsistent parameter domains.

Feature-driven methods answer where additional freedom is useful. Smoothed
discrete curvature \cite{li2005}, dominant-point refinement \cite{park2007},
and derivative-derived feature distributions \cite{yeh2020} provide fast and
geometrically interpretable placement rules. Their final knot number,
however, is usually set by a rule or repeated refinement. We adopt their
feature-awareness in the proposal stage: coordinates and first- and
second-order differences are encoded in traversal order, and local
cross-attention over-generates candidates before the final count is decided.

Optimization-driven methods address how many knots should remain more
directly. Error-bounded insertion \cite{liang2017} responds explicitly to the
current residual; continuous knot optimization \cite{dung2017} can refine
locations at a prescribed order; sparse selection \cite{kang2015} exposes the
contribution of an overcomplete basis; and an $\ell_{\infty,1}$ model combined
with differential evolution \cite{luo2022} explores knot combinations more
globally. These are useful but may require sequential refits, a prescribed
model order, or expensive iterative search. Our selector amortizes the subset
decision across curves and then relocates the survivors, combining a parallel
discrete prediction with continuous coordinate correction.

Classical knot insertion and removal provide exact refinement or
error-controlled simplification operators for an existing spline
\cite{boehm1980,tiller1992}. Lyche and M{\o}rken~\cite{lyche1988} formulate
the accuracy--complexity balance as data reduction under a prescribed
perturbation tolerance. Their key benefit for this work is explicit
feasibility checking. Our complete deployment system follows that principle:
residual insertion and verified deletion both refit the controls and accept an
edit only when the MSE and maximum pointwise-error bounds are satisfied.
Because all control points are recomputed after an edit, the wrapper is not a
verbatim implementation of the classical shape-preserving operators.

\subsection{Surface fitting and geometry-aware parameterization}
Curve and surface fitting share the dependence on parameterization and local
representation capacity. Lai et al.~\cite{lai2006} construct a
feature-sensitive surface parameterization that assigns more parameter space
to highly curved regions, allowing uniform knots to induce a nonuniform
allocation of control variables. Our parameter-position encoding and local
attention serve the analogous purpose of linking a geometric event to its
parameter-domain capacity. Kineri et al.~\cite{kineri2012} use iterative
geometric interpolation and approximation to update a B-spline surface after
an initial model has been chosen; correspondingly, our retained knots are
updated after the KeepMask changes their neighbourhoods. Seiler et
al.~\cite{seiler2018} combine point and normal data to control localized
surface quality, illustrating why a global average may hide poor local
behaviour. We do not use normal constraints, but explicitly accompany MSE
with a maximum pointwise squared-error bound. These surface studies provide
design principles rather than direct baseline algorithms for our
one-dimensional subset problem.

\subsection{Learning-based parameterization and knot prediction}
The principal advantage of learning is to amortize per-curve structure search
over a data distribution. Interdependent predictors for parameters and knots
\cite{laube2018}, residual parameterization networks \cite{scholz2021},
supervised parameter selection \cite{song2024}, and per-instance neural
parameter solvers \cite{tang2025} establish that geometric correspondence can
be learned. They mainly address continuous parameters or predictions under a
fixed structure. We place parameter prediction and knot selection in one data
flow and update the parameters again from survivor memory after the subset has
been chosen.

For knot structure, pointwise SVM scores from geometric and
differential-geometric descriptors provide interpretable local evidence
\cite{laube2018svm}. Unsupervised per-instance neural optimization
\cite{wen2024}, Transformer knot-vector prediction \cite{saillot2024}, the
autoregressive variable-length SplineGen decoder \cite{zou2025}, explicit
parameter--knot co-optimization \cite{tang2026}, and point-relative matrix
representations for length and multiplicity \cite{saillot2026} demonstrate
increasingly flexible structure prediction. Autoregression, per-instance
optimization, or independent local scores can nevertheless introduce
sequential cost or omit how deletion changes the relationships among the
remaining knots. We instead use a parallel fixed-capacity proposal, one hard
KeepMask, and a survivor-conditioned decoder whose attention memory and
neighbour geometry change with the selected combination.

Two adjacent lines clarify the output of our pipeline. NURBS-Diff
\cite{prasad2022} supplies differentiable curve and surface evaluation but
does not solve the discrete minimum-cardinality subset. InceptCurves
\cite{barzegar2024} predicts control-point number and coordinates for uniform
B-spline or subdivision reconstruction. Our network predicts nonuniform
internal-knot structure and leaves controls to least squares. The contribution
is therefore not the first neural knot predictor; it is the coupling of
high-recall proposal, curve-level selection, and KeepMask-conditioned
relocation under joint mean/peak sampled-error feasibility.

\subsection{Position of the present work}
The preceding methods offer complementary strengths: feature rules provide
fast and interpretable placement priors; residual insertion provides direct
feasibility feedback; continuous optimization refines coordinates; sparse
models compress an overcomplete basis; and learning amortizes repeated search.
Our three-stage network organizes these ideas as high-recall proposal,
MSE-conditioned subset selection, and survivor relocation. The complete
deployed system then uses a standard B-spline refit and explicit dual-bound
error verification.
The method does not claim a global minimum-cardinality certificate.

The repository implementations of the five numerical baselines share the
input, knot budget, and evaluation definitions used in this study, but not
every native objective, continuity model, or solver detail. Their measured
values are therefore comparisons among disclosed adaptations rather than exact
reproductions of the original publications. The bounded insertion/deletion
evaluation wrapper is separately disclosed and is not folded into the claimed
network architecture.

\section{Problem and error definitions}
The input is a normalized sequence of observations
$\bm Q=(\bm q_i)_{i=1}^M$, $\bm q_i\in\mathbb R^d$, in traversal order. We
seek an open-clamped B-spline of degree $p=3$. For ordered internal knots
$0<u_1<\cdots<u_K<1$, the complete knot vector is
\begin{equation}
\bm U=(\xi_r)_{r=1}^{K+8}
=(0,0,0,0,u_1,\ldots,u_K,1,1,1,1),
\end{equation}
and the curve is
\begin{equation}
\bm C(t)=\sum_{a=1}^{K+4}B_{a,3}(t;\bm U)\bm P_a,\qquad t\in[0,1].
\label{eq:bspline-curve}
\end{equation}
The control vertices are $\bm P_a\in\mathbb R^d$. The basis is defined by the
Cox--de Boor recursion \cite{piegl1997}:
\begin{equation}
B_{a,0}(t)=
\begin{cases}
1,& \xi_a\leq t<\xi_{a+1},\\
0,& \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:bspline-zero}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
B_{a,p}(t)={}&
\frac{t-\xi_a}{\xi_{a+p}-\xi_a}B_{a,p-1}(t)\\
&+\frac{\xi_{a+p+1}-t}{\xi_{a+p+1}-\xi_{a+1}}B_{a+1,p-1}(t),
\qquad p=1,2,3,
\end{split}
\label{eq:cox-de-boor}
\end{equation}
where a term with a zero denominator is defined as zero and the usual endpoint
convention is used at $t=1$. Each cubic basis function has local support
$[\xi_a,\xi_{a+4})$; the bases are nonnegative and form a partition of unity.
Moving or deleting an internal knot therefore alters nearby basis supports as
well as the associated least-squares system. This local but combinatorial
effect is the mathematical reason that subset selection and survivor
relocation should not be predicted independently.

The observations do not arrive with parameters. We must also estimate the
strictly ordered correspondence
\begin{equation}
0=t_1<t_2<\cdots<t_M=1.
\label{eq:parameter-order}
\end{equation}
For fixed $\bm t=(t_i)$ and $\bm U$, the spline is linear in its control
vertices. $K$ counts internal knots only. Every additional simple internal
knot in a cubic spline adds one control vertex and one local degree of freedom;
thus $K$ measures capacity, storage, and downstream editing complexity.

The deployed solver consequently uses the standard endpoint-constrained
least-squares problem
\begin{equation}
\bm P^\star=\underset{\bm P}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\|\bm C(t_i)-\bm q_i\|_2^2,\qquad
\bm P_1=\bm q_1,\quad \bm P_{K+4}=\bm q_M .
\label{eq:control-ls}
\end{equation}
The learned component can thus focus on the nonlinear structural variables---
the data parameters, knot count, and knot positions---while the controls remain
the output of a standard, inspectable numerical solve
\cite{piegl1997,yeh2020}.

Minimizing an average residual alone can conceal an isolated severe deviation.
We therefore distinguish overall fidelity from localized failure through the
sampled squared residual, its mean, and its maximum:
\begin{equation}
e_i=\|\bm C(t_i)-\bm q_i\|_2^2,\qquad
\MSE=M^{-1}\sum_i e_i,\qquad \Peak=\max_i e_i.
\end{equation}
Neither metric is square-rooted. MSE describes average approximation quality,
whereas $\Peak$ prevents many small residuals from hiding a local error spike.
Given tolerances $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ and a maximum candidate capacity
$K_c$, the compact-fitting objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{K,\bm t,\bm U}\quad &K\\
\text{subject to}\quad
&0\leq K\leq K_c,\\
&0=t_1<\cdots<t_M=1,\qquad 0<u_1<\cdots<u_K<1,\\
&\MSE(\bm t,\bm U,\bm P^\star)\leq\varepsilon,\\
&\Peak(\bm t,\bm U,\bm P^\star)\leq\delta,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $\bm P^\star$ is recomputed for the current parameters and knots through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:control-ls}. Feasibility has priority: a reduction in $K$ is not
accepted if either sampled bound is violated.

Equation~\eqref{eq:target} combines a discrete subset, continuous
parameterization, continuous knot relocation, and a nested control-point
solve. Rather than attempting a global free-knot optimization, we learn a
high-recall overcomplete proposal, predict a KeepMask, and condition parameter
and knot updates on the survivors. For the reported checkpoint, the teacher
and selector use $\MSE\leq\varepsilon$ as their training feasibility test,
with auxiliary local-region supervision to discourage concentrated residuals.
The maximum-error bound $\Peak\leq\delta$ enters the complete deployment
system explicitly: a violation of either bound invokes residual insertion, a
deletion is accepted only when both remain satisfied, and final success also
requires both. Thus peak error is an operational system constraint rather than
an extra reporting column, but it is not presented as a hard guarantee learned
by the historical network weights.

The errors above are corresponding-point errors evaluated only at the input
samples. They are not Hausdorff distances and do not certify the continuous
parameter intervals between observations. Endpoint closure and periodicity
are output-model conventions and are discussed with the spline solver in
Section~\ref{sec:spline-output}.

\section{Framework overview}
\subsection{Task decomposition}
The target in Eq.~\eqref{eq:target} mixes a discrete decision (which knots to
retain) with continuous variables (point parameters and knot positions); the
control vertices are solved only after those structural variables have been
fixed. Directly regressing a short knot vector creates two problems: its output
dimension changes with curve complexity, and an erroneous early count decision
cannot be repaired by later coordinate prediction. At the other extreme,
retaining a large fixed knot vector gives low residuals but does not produce a
compact representation.

The proposed network separates this coupled problem into proposal, selection,
and survivor-conditioned refinement. The proposal path deliberately
over-generates ordered candidates to preserve recall. The selection path turns
candidate evidence into a curve-level cardinality and a hard KeepMask. The
refinement path then treats that mask as part of the geometry: only retained
tokens define the neighbourhoods and attention memory used to update point
parameters and knot locations. A conventional least-squares solve converts the
network output into control points. Thus the network predicts spline structure
and coordinates, but does not replace the standard spline solver.

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} separates the learned deployment path from
training-only supervision. The upper path makes one discrete subset decision:
the same KeepMask then conditions the parameter and survivor-knot updates
before a single endpoint-constrained B-spline refit. The dashed lower path
provides synthetic parameter/knot labels and counterfactual subset targets
during training but is absent from deployment. Residual insertion and verified
deletion used by the threshold experiments are intentionally omitted because
they belong to the evaluation wrapper rather than the proposed network.

\subsection{Coupled three-stage inference}
The objective in Section~3 contains two competing requirements. Candidate
knots must first cover parameter regions that may otherwise produce large
residuals, because no downstream selector can recover a local degree of
freedom that was never proposed. Retaining every candidate, however, defeats
the minimum-complexity objective. Moreover, deleting a knot immediately
changes survivor neighbourhoods, basis supports, and the appropriate point
parameters and knot coordinates. Proposal, cardinality selection, and
coordinate refinement should therefore not be implemented as unrelated output
heads.

We organize inference according to ``establish a feasible search space,
compress the structure, and then recover geometric adaptability after
deletion.'' The proposal stage favours recall and supplies enough alternative
locations for accurate fitting. The selection stage allocates curve-level
capacity and makes one subset decision. The relocation stage treats that
subset as new geometric context and corrects the sample parameters and
surviving knots. This ordering follows the priority of the constrained problem:
avoid irreversible proposal omissions, remove redundancy, and finally
compensate the continuous variables for the discrete edit. The three stages
are consequently successive responsibilities within one coupled inference
path, not three independent predictors.

Figure~\ref{fig:stage-coupling} makes the interfaces explicit. Stage I exports
state $S_1=\{\bm F,\bm t^{(p)},\bm U^{(p)},\bm h,\varepsilon\}$: point memory,
the common parameter frame, and an ordered but deliberately redundant
candidate state. Stage II consumes $S_1$ and exports
$S_2=\{\bm m,K,\bm h_{\rm surv}\}$, where one KeepMask simultaneously fixes
the cardinality, the survivor sequence, and the memory visible downstream.
Stage III consumes $S_2$ together with the point memory and exports
$S_3=\{\bm t',\bm U'[\bm m]\}$, a strictly ordered variable-length knot
representation. The explicit CAD solve then reads the original observations
and $S_3$; it does not read rejected candidate tokens or teacher labels.

\subsubsection{Stage I: order-aware parameter encoding and redundant proposal}
The first stage reads the complete ordered sample sequence. The
GeometryEncoder maps coordinates and chord-normalized first- and second-order
difference cues to point features $\bm F$. In parallel, the ParameterHead uses
chord length as a stable reference and predicts an initial strictly increasing
parameterization $\bm t^{(p)}$. Parameter-position encoding is then written
back to the point features so that a subsequent query can identify both a
geometric event and its location in $[0,1]$.

The CandidateKnotHead applies $K_c+1$ ordered interval queries and local
Gaussian-biased cross-attention to produce $K_c$ ordered candidate coordinates
$\bm U^{(p)}$ and candidate tokens $\bm h$. $K_c$ is proposal capacity, not the
final model order. Controlled over-generation is intentional: this stage is
trained to cover potentially useful regions, since a region missed by the
proposal cannot be recovered by the selector. Its interface $S_1$ is therefore
$(\bm F,\bm t^{(p)},\bm U^{(p)},\bm h,\varepsilon)$---evidence for where knots
may be useful, the parameter frame in which those locations are interpreted,
and the requested fitting condition. It is not yet a final knot vector.

\subsubsection{Stage II: curve-level capacity and one KeepMask}
The second stage lets candidate tokens interact and combines them with local
point evidence, parameter-domain coverage, and the requested MSE tolerance.
The selector outputs relative candidate scores and a curve-conditioned offset
$\beta$. Rather than applying a fixed probability threshold independently to
all candidates, it aggregates probability mass to estimate a requested count
and uses a stable Top-$K$ selection with coarse coverage anchors. The
experimental protocol uses $\delta=10\varepsilon$, so the same request fixes
both feasibility bounds. The reported Selector is trained directly under the
MSE condition; the complete deployment wrapper applies the peak bound to the
selected combination through residual insertion, verified deletion, and final
joint-bound validation rather than through an independent candidate score.

This operation produces one discrete decision $\bm m$, the KeepMask. The
resulting interface $S_2$ contains $\bm m$, its count $K$, and the ordered
survivor tokens. The same mask jointly determines output cardinality,
neighbour relations, attention memory, and the coordinates extracted after
Stage III; no second, inconsistent mask is formed. The selector should
therefore be interpreted as curve-level capacity allocation followed by
ranked location selection, not as $K_c$ unrelated binary classifiers.

\subsubsection{Stage III: survivor-conditioned parameter and knot update}
Selection is not treated as the end of coordinate prediction. The mask first
changes the geometry seen by the refinement module: left/right gaps, survivor
rank, and selected count are recomputed, and deleted candidates are excluded
from the attention Key/Value memory. The selected-node decoder can thus
exchange information only within the final survivor set.

Point-parameter queries read the same survivor memory to obtain a monotone
parameter correction. Candidate knots follow the corresponding parameter
warp and then receive a bounded, neighbourhood-constrained residual update.
Consequently, when a knot is deleted, its neighbours can redistribute across
the newly exposed interval while point parameters and knot coordinates remain
in a shared parameter system. Stage III exports
$S_3=(\bm t',\bm U'[\bm m])$; one standard solve then computes the controls.
Because rejected tokens cannot re-enter the Key/Value memory, this update is
conditional on the deployed structure rather than a refinement of the
original dense proposal. The three stages therefore divide responsibility:
proposal favours recall, selection supplies compression, and survivor
refinement compensates for the geometry changed by deletion.

\subsection{Data flow and training/deployment boundary}
For a batch of ordered points $\bm Q\in\mathbb R^{B\times M\times d}$, the
forward path is
\begin{equation}
\bm Q\rightarrow(\bm F,\bm t^{(p)})\rightarrow
(\bm U^{(p)},\bm h)\rightarrow
(\bm m,\bm t',\bm U')\rightarrow
\operatorname{BSplineRefit}(\bm Q,\bm t',\bm U').
\label{eq:pipeline}
\end{equation}
Here $\bm F$ denotes point features, $\bm U^{(p)}$ the redundant proposals,
$\bm h$ their candidate tokens, $\bm m$ the single discrete subset decision,
and $\bm U'$ the relocated surviving knots. The arrows express information
dependence: the selector reads both point and candidate evidence; the
refinement module reads the mask, candidate tokens, and initial
parameterization; and the spline solver reads only the final parameters,
knots, and observations.

Proposal and Joint denote training schedules, not two networks executed in
sequence at deployment. Proposal training first establishes monotone
parameters and high-recall candidates. Joint training then learns curve-level
capacity, the KeepMask, and survivor-conditioned updates. Inference follows
the single upper path in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} and performs one final
B-spline refit. Synthetic parameter/knot labels and counterfactual subset
teacher signals occur only during training. The residual insertion/deletion
wrapper used in comparative threshold experiments is also outside this
forward path; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-wrapper} states its scope.

\section{Network architecture}

\subsection{Order-aware geometric encoding}
The observations are samples of a curve in traversal order, not an unordered
point cloud. The encoder should therefore preserve adjacency and distinguish
the same spatial point reached with different local tangents. After curve-level
normalization, let
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bm q_i=\bm q_{i+1}-\bm q_i,\qquad
\ell_i=\|\Delta\bm q_i\|_2,
\qquad h_i=\frac{\ell_i}{\sum_l\ell_l}.
\end{equation}
The first derivative proxy divides each segment by its normalized chord span,
\begin{equation}
\bm d_i=\frac{\Delta\bm q_i}{h_i},
\end{equation}
and the interior second derivative proxy is
\begin{equation}
\bm a_i=\frac{\bm d_i-\bm d_{i-1}}
{\tfrac12(h_i+h_{i-1})}.
\end{equation}
Boundary channels use their implemented one-sided/zero extensions. The input
at sample $i$ is the concatenation
\begin{equation}
\bm x_i=[\bm q_i,\bm d_i,\bm a_i].
\end{equation}

These channels have complementary roles. Normalized coordinates retain the
coarse curve shape and endpoint configuration; $\bm d_i$ represents local
tangent and parameter-domain speed; and $\bm a_i$ responds to changes in
tangent, providing a discrete bending cue. Dividing by chord spans reduces the
dependence of both derivative channels on nonuniform sampling density. It does
not turn them into exact differential invariants: noise and resampling still
affect the estimates, which is why the network reads all three channels rather
than using curvature alone. This design is consistent with classical evidence
that derivative-derived feature content is informative for allocating knots
to geometrically detailed regions \cite{yeh2020}; here those cues are learned
jointly rather than converted into a fixed placement rule.

Three width-128 one-dimensional convolution blocks with kernel size three map
the ordered sequence to local features $\bm F=(\bm f_i)_{i=1}^M$. Each block
uses GroupNorm and GELU. A 1D convolution is chosen because its receptive field
respects consecutive curve samples, while GroupNorm does not rely on large
batch statistics \cite{wu2018}. Global max pooling gives a curve descriptor
\begin{equation}
\bm f_g=\max_i\bm f_i,
\end{equation}
which conveys whether a local pattern occurs anywhere on the curve without
discarding the pointwise sequence needed by later attention.

\subsection{Chord-anchored parameter encoding}
Knot coordinates live in parameter space, so spatial features alone are
insufficient for candidate queries. We first construct the chord-length
reference
\begin{equation}
c_1=0,\qquad
c_i=\frac{\sum_{l<i}\ell_l}{\sum_l\ell_l},\qquad c_M=1.
\end{equation}
The ParameterHead reads $[\bm f_i,\bm f_g]$ and predicts bounded residual
logits for the $M-1$ parameter gaps. For minimum gap $\eta_t$, let
$g_i^c=c_{i+1}-c_i$ and $r_i$ be a bounded, mean-centred logit. The strict
gaps are
\begin{equation}
g_i=\eta_t+
[1-(M-1)\eta_t]
\frac{(g_i^c-\eta_t)_+\exp(r_i)}
{\sum_l(g_l^c-\eta_t)_+\exp(r_l)}.
\label{eq:parameter-gaps}
\end{equation}
Cumulative summation fixes $t_1=0$ and $t_M=1$ and guarantees strict
monotonicity by construction. A learned curve-level trust factor blends this
proposal back toward the strict chord reference. Chord length is used as an
anchor because it is deterministic, order-preserving, and usually a reasonable
initial parameterization; the residual path remains necessary because equal
spatial travel does not imply equal approximation difficulty. The sensitivity
of fitted shape to the parameter nodes is well established
\cite{lee1989,scholz2021}, which motivates predicting a bounded correction
instead of treating chord length as final.

Finally, sinusoidal parameter encoding is added to every local feature,
\begin{equation}
\bm M_i=\bm f_i+\operatorname{PE}(t_i),
\end{equation}
and $\bm M$ becomes the point memory for candidate cross-attention. This step
expresses geometry and location in a common coordinate system: a knot query can
now ask both \emph{what} geometric event occurs and \emph{where} it occurs in
$[0,1]$. The query--key--value computation and sinusoidal encoding follow the
general attention formulation of Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017}; the
parameter-domain Gaussian bias and ordered interval queries are specific to
our curve setting. Ground-truth knots are not inputs to either encoder or
ParameterHead.

\subsection{Candidate generation}
Candidate queries use local Gaussian-biased cross-attention to read
parameter-positioned point features. The CandidateKnotHead produces
$K_c$ ordered interior coordinates $\bm U^{(p)}$ and tokens $\bm h_j$.
These are learned proposals, not necessarily exact shape-preserving Boehm
insertions. An explicit redundant input spline is not required at deployment.
The deliberate over-generation is a recall mechanism: missing a geometrically
important region cannot be corrected by a selector, whereas an unnecessary
candidate can still be removed later.
For one attention head, the local geometric readout has the form
\begin{equation}
 A_{ji}=\operatorname{softmax}_{i}\left(
 \frac{(W_Qh_j)^\top(W_KF_i)}{\sqrt{d_h}}
 -\frac{(t_i-u_j)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\qquad
 \widehat h_j=\sum_i A_{ji}W_VF_i.
\end{equation}
Positional encodings, learned projections, residual connections, and
normalization are included in the implementation. The Gaussian term is a
locality bias, not a guarantee that attention finds curvature extrema.

\subsection{Adaptive selection and variable cardinality}
Candidate interaction combines point memory, neighborhood coverage features,
and an MSE-tolerance embedding. With importance scores $a_j$ and a curve-level
adaptive threshold $\beta$, the profile uses
\begin{equation}
p_j=\sigma(a_j-\bar a-\beta),\qquad m=\sum_{j=1}^{K_c}p_j.
\end{equation}
For zero safety reserve, requested cardinality is zero if $m<0.5$, and
$\lceil m\rceil$ otherwise, clipped to its admissible range. In the current
profile this range is $[4,K_c]$, so a zero raw request still retains at least
four knots. The highest-probability candidate in each of four nonempty
parameter bins is given priority when the requested count can accommodate
all bin anchors; remaining slots follow the probability ranking. KeepMask
is obtained by one stable Top-$K$ operation on this adjusted ranking. Thus independent
$p_j\geq0.5$ tests are \emph{not} this profile's selection rule. Optional
reserve settings and changes to the four-bin coverage rule must be disclosed
separately. The mask supplies the output length; separate decoders for every
possible $K$ are not evaluated. This makes cardinality a curve-level decision
rather than $K_c$ unrelated binary classifications. In the current protocol,
$\delta=10\varepsilon$, so the input $\varepsilon$ indexes a unique pair of
mean and peak requirements. The selector does not use a separate MaxSE
classifier; the complete deployment wrapper applies $\delta$ to the selected
combination during residual repair and deletion acceptance.

\subsection{KeepMask-conditioned survivor interaction}
Let $m_j\in\{0,1\}$ denote the KeepMask and
$K=\sum_jm_j$. For every candidate position $u_j$, define its nearest retained
neighbours
\begin{align}
u_j^-&=\max\bigl(\{0\}\cup\{u_l:l<j,\ m_l=1\}\bigr),\\
u_j^+&=\min\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{u_l:l>j,\ m_l=1\}\bigr),
\end{align}
and its retained rank
\begin{equation}
\rho_j=\frac{\sum_{l\leq j}m_l}{K+1}.
\end{equation}
The mask-conditioned geometry vector is
\begin{equation}
\bm g_j=\left[u_j-u_j^-,\ u_j^+-u_j,\ \rho_j,\ K/K_c\right].
\label{eq:keep-geometry}
\end{equation}
An MLP embeds $\bm g_j$ and adds it to the original candidate token,
\begin{equation}
\bm z_j=\bm h_j+\operatorname{MLP}_{g}(\bm g_j).
\end{equation}
This is what we mean by a \emph{KeepMask-conditioned embedding}: it is not a
two-entry lookup table for ``keep'' and ``discard''. Its value changes when any
neighbour is deleted because the distances, retained rank, and retained count
must be recomputed for the new subset.

The survivor memory contains only retained tokens,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal S=\{\bm z_j\mid m_j=1\}.
\end{equation}
In the batched implementation all $K_c$ storage slots remain present, but a
key-padding mask prevents discarded candidates from serving as attention keys
or values. A zero sentinel is exposed only for an empty subset. Survivor tokens
are updated by
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\bm z}_j=\operatorname{LN}\left(
\bm z_j+\operatorname{Attn}(\bm z_j,\mathcal S,\mathcal S)\right),
\end{equation}
and parameter-point queries attend to the same memory,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\bm f}_i=\operatorname{LN}\left(
\bm p_i+\operatorname{Attn}(\bm p_i,\mathcal S,\mathcal S)\right),
\qquad
\bm p_i=\bm f_i+\operatorname{PE}(t_i)+\bm e_{\varepsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\bm e_{\varepsilon}$ is the requested MSE-tolerance embedding; under
the present $\delta=10\varepsilon$ protocol it also uniquely identifies the
corresponding peak-error target.

This construction serves four purposes. First, it prevents a discarded token
from leaking geometric evidence into the final decoder through attention.
Second, it replaces proposal-grid spacing by the actual spacing of the retained
subset, including intervals spanning several deleted slots. Third, retained
rank and count tell the decoder whether the same local feature belongs to a
sparse or dense final representation. Fourth, point-to-survivor attention lets
the hard structural decision modify parameterization before knot relocation.
Consequently, KeepMask determines not only which coordinates are exported but
also how the surviving coordinates are interpreted and updated.

The dependence is combinatorial: a candidate judged redundant in the complete
proposal may become necessary after a neighbour disappears. Training therefore
uses the same subset decoder for predicted and teacher masks; supervising a
mask without redecoding its survivor geometry would not match deployment.

\subsection{Coupled parameter and knot refinement}
The selected subset is also evidence for parameterization. Let
$g_i=t^{(p)}_{i+1}-t^{(p)}_i$ and let $r_i$ be the decoder correction for the
$i$th parameter interval. With minimum parameter gap $\eta_t$, the free
interval mass is redistributed as
\begin{equation}
w_i=\frac{(g_i-\eta_t)_+\exp(\bar r_i)}
{\sum_l(g_l-\eta_t)_+\exp(\bar r_l)},\qquad
\widehat g_i=\eta_t+[1-(M-1)\eta_t]w_i,
\end{equation}
where $\bar r_i$ is a bounded, mean-centred version of $r_i$. Cumulative sums
give a monotone proposal $\widehat{\bm t}$, and a learned curve-level trust
$\gamma\in[0,1]$ produces
\begin{equation}
\bm t'=\bm t^{(p)}+\gamma(\widehat{\bm t}-\bm t^{(p)}).
\end{equation}

Changing point parameters without moving knots would make their coordinates
inconsistent. Candidate knots are therefore transported through the same
piecewise-linear map
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W(u;\bm t,\bm t')=
 t'_i+\frac{u-t_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i}(t'_{i+1}-t'_i),
 \quad u\in[t_i,t_{i+1}],
\end{equation}
and the survivor decoder predicts a bounded displacement using its retained
rank and neighbouring survivors. A final ordered-gap projection enforces
$0<u'_1<\cdots<u'_K<1$ with the prescribed minimum gap. Thus deletion and
coordinate adjustment share the same survivor representation instead of being
two independent predictions. The evaluated checkpoint performs this coupled
decode once; it does not contain the later experimental repeated-refinement
blocks. The warp aligns coordinate systems but is not an exact
shape-preserving reparameterization of a cubic spline.

\subsection{Spline output}
\label{sec:spline-output}
The retained coordinates form the interior of an open-clamped cubic knot
vector. Given the predicted parameters and knots, one endpoint-constrained
double-precision least-squares solve in Eq.~\eqref{eq:control-ls} computes the
control points. The network does not directly regress these controls. For an
input with identical first and last observations, the two fixed endpoint
controls therefore coincide and produce positional closure, but the solver
does not convert the curve into a periodic spline or enforce derivative
continuity across that seam. This is the raw output of the proposed network
and the endpoint of the architectural description.

For threshold-based comparisons, we additionally record a conventional
post-fit wrapper. If either MSE or MaxSE violates its bound, the wrapper can
insert a knot at a high-residual location; it can also test whether a
single-knot deletion preserves both bounds. It contains no learned parameters and is
not presented as a network component; its use, timing, and asymmetry across
methods are stated in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-wrapper}.

\section{Training}
\subsection{Why proposal and selection are trained in stages}
The proposal stage is optimized first because selection labels are useful only
when the candidate set already covers the important parameter regions. It
emphasizes dense fitting, monotone parameter prediction, ordered assignment to
labelled synthetic knots, and high candidate recall. The joint stage then
activates the adaptive selector and subset decoder. Its initial calibration
interval freezes proposal geometry so that the selector cannot improve its
loss merely by moving the candidate set; proposal parameters are subsequently
unfrozen at a smaller learning rate. This curriculum follows the causal order
of the task: generate plausible alternatives before learning which alternatives
can be removed.

\subsection{Counterfactual teacher for discrete selection}
Hard Top-$K$ selection is discrete, and a source knot vector is not necessarily
the smallest subset that satisfies the chosen sampled-error tolerance after
refitting. During joint training, a bounded numerical teacher therefore tests
stochastic subsets, ranked prefixes, and limited add/delete/swap edits on
selected synthetic curves. Its purpose is to answer a local counterfactual:
``could this curve remain below the training MSE bound with a smaller or
different subset?'' It is
not a separate neural network, an offline label cache, or a globally optimal
oracle.

A mask changes the survivor-conditioned decoder, so fixed-geometry deletion is
not sufficient for a training label. Every candidate teacher mask is decoded
again and refitted before it may supervise feasibility or count. For the
reported checkpoint, teacher feasibility is $\MSE\leq\varepsilon$, augmented
by local-region and endpoint fit supervision to discourage concentrated
residuals. This training label must not be confused with a MaxSE certificate:
$\Peak\leq\delta$ is checked explicitly by the numerical deployment wrapper
during residual insertion and verified deletion. All teacher decisions are
detached; no ordinary derivative through hard Top-$K$ is assumed.

\subsection{Grouped objective}
The two stages use related but distinct objectives. In compact notation,
\begin{align}
\mathcal L_{\rm prop}={}&
\lambda_d\mathcal L_{\rm dense}
+\lambda_t\mathcal L_{\rm parameter}
+\lambda_v\mathcal L_{\rm coverage}
+\lambda_u\mathcal L_{\rm ordered\ position},\\
\mathcal L_{\rm joint}={}&
\lambda_f\mathcal L_{\rm selected\ fit}
+\lambda_m\mathcal L_{\rm mask}
+\lambda_c\mathcal L_{\rm count}
+\lambda_r\mathcal L_{\rm rank}
+\lambda_g\mathcal L_{\rm teacher\ geometry}
+\lambda_K\mathcal L_{\rm complexity}.
\end{align}
The selected-fit term penalizes subset MSE relative to $\varepsilon$. An
auxiliary local-fit term also evaluates four parameter bins and two endpoint
regions, reducing the chance that a poor region is completely hidden by the
global average. Mask, count, and ranking terms distil counterfactual choices
after re-decoding and MSE verification; parameter, coverage, and position
losses use ground truth only for labelled synthetic samples. The complexity
term activates only with training feasibility evidence.

The codebase additionally supports an optional exact-maximum/upper-tail
pointwise loss, but its weight is zero in the reported K32 checkpoint. It is
therefore not attributed to the measured network. In the complete evaluated
system, $\delta$ enters through the numerical wrapper: failure of either MSE
or MaxSE triggers residual insertion, and a deletion is accepted only when
both bounds remain satisfied. This distinction integrates peak error into the
deployed optimization and acceptance rule without overstating the historical
training objective. Neither stage proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:target}.
The exact enabled implementation terms are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:loss}.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\subsection{Training data and selected model}
Training uses normalized two-dimensional curves with $M=192$ ordered points.
The synthetic mixture contains simple analytic shapes, procedurally perturbed
shapes, and labelled cubic B-splines; only the latter provide true parameter
and source-knot supervision. The selected model was trained with 1500 generated
curves per epoch and 500 synthetic validation curves, with Gaussian coordinate
noise of standard deviation $10^{-3}$. No external real curve is used as a
labelled training sample.

The evaluated checkpoint has $K_c=32$, hidden width 128, three encoder blocks,
four attention heads, and two selector blocks. It was selected at epoch 14
from a run configured for 12 proposal and 20 joint epochs. This is a historical
warm-started checkpoint, and the initializer chain is retained in the audit
metadata. The deployed architecture uses one survivor-conditioned decode and
no additional repeated-refinement block. These facts are reported to prevent
later, unmeasured architectural variants from being attributed to the results.

\subsection{Paired test cohort}
The study additionally includes training/validation monitoring, repeated model
development, tolerance sweeps, and case analyses. To make every six-method
comparison curve-wise paired, the main aggregate table freezes an audit subset
of 61 curves: 21 synthetic B-splines with source
internal counts from 4 to 24, and 10 curves each from UJI Pen Characters
Version 2~\cite{uji2008}, Natural Earth coastlines~\cite{naturalearth}, USGS
contours~\cite{usgscontours}, and procedural industrial-model offsets. The
last source is CAD-style generated geometry, not field-measured industrial
data. Every method receives the same normalized observations, and every
attempted curve remains in the pass-rate denominator. The cohort is a fixed
paired study, not 10\% of each complete source.

External sources do not provide ground-truth spline knot vectors. Consequently,
knot precision and recall are reported only for synthetic curves under a shared
parameterization; external curves are evaluated by sampled fitting error,
complexity, and time. Upsampling short records does not create independent
measurements.

\subsection{Evaluation wrapper and fairness scope}
\label{sec:evaluation-wrapper}
Ours is compared with five disclosed repository adaptations: Park--Lee,
Liang, Dung, Kang, and Luo. They share the same normalized input and maximum
of 32 internal knots, but retain their documented parameterization and
control-point conventions. In particular, Dung and Kang use their existing
unconstrained control-point paths, whereas Ours uses endpoint-constrained
least squares. The comparison must therefore be described as an implementation
study, not an exact reproduction of the authors' original software.

For the threshold-based comparison, every raw method is followed by the same
additive residual-insertion wrapper. It keeps existing parameters and knots
fixed and refits the control points after each insertion. Ours additionally
uses verified single-knot deletion after reaching the sampled bounds; the five
adaptations do not. Neither operation is a learned network layer. This
asymmetry and all associated time are retained in the reported protocol rather
than folded into the architecture claim. Although insertion and removal are
classical spline operations \cite{boehm1980,tiller1992}, this wrapper performs
a complete refit after a residual-guided edit and should not be confused with
shape-preserving Boehm insertion or a verbatim implementation of Tiller's
removal algorithm.

\subsection{Metrics and timing}
The principal setting is $\varepsilon=5\times10^{-5}$ and
$\delta=5\times10^{-4}$. A curve passes only if both sampled bounds hold.
We report the mean internal-knot count over finite fits, joint pass rate over
all requested curves, mean MSE, mean and worst per-curve $\Peak$, and total
latency. A numerical exception remains a failure rather than receiving zero
error. Total latency includes the raw method, residual insertion, and, for
Ours, verified deletion. Network-only latency is supplementary. Because neural
inference uses a GPU while baseline optimization is primarily CPU-based, the
recorded times describe the tested system and are not a hardware-neutral
complexity comparison.

Sensitivity is evaluated at
$\varepsilon\in\{10^{-5},2\times10^{-5},5\times10^{-5},10^{-4}\}$ with
$\delta=10\varepsilon$, using the same checkpoint and paired cohort at every
level. Thus the sweep measures deployment sensitivity, not four separately
trained optimal networks.

\section{Limitations and validity}
Raw one-shot fits can fail the tolerance and retain too many knots. Candidate
capacity and proposal quality limit performance before selection. The
comparison wrapper increases runtime, and its Ours-only deletion stage makes
the principal six-method protocol asymmetric; raw, insertion-only, and
wrapper-complete ablations are needed to attribute gains correctly.
Teacher verification covers only selected training curves and does not
guarantee a feasible learned policy. Distribution shift and parameter bias
remain visible on coastline and contour data. The paired inference ablation
also shows that adaptive mass-Top-$K$ is not uniformly better than a fixed
$0.5$ mask, so selector calibration remains unresolved.

Both constraints are checked only at the 192 supplied observation parameters.
No global knot minimality, Hausdorff bound, or continuous-curve error
certificate is claimed. The fixed paired audit subset is intentionally
controlled but small, although the broader study also contains repeated
training, validation, and tolerance experiments. Timings mix GPU inference
with CPU double-precision numerical solves and should not be interpreted as
universal hardware speedups.

Baseline adaptations show some severe original-reference instability despite
small input-grid residuals. Such failures need investigation and disclosure,
not silent removal. Procedural offsets do not establish general performance
on measured industrial geometry.

The procedural offsets repeat their first observation and are therefore
positionally closed by the endpoint constraints in Eq.~\eqref{eq:control-ls}.
This should not be
interpreted as support for periodic closed curves. The encoder uses non-cyclic
boundary features, the knot vector is open-clamped, and first- and
second-derivative equality at the seam is not enforced. Extending the proposal,
KeepMask neighbourhoods, parameterization, and control solve to a genuinely
periodic formulation is future work.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a learning-guided framework for compact cubic B-spline fitting
from ordered samples. An order-aware encoder combines coordinates with
chord-normalized derivative cues; a local cross-attention proposal network
predicts parameter-aware redundant knots; adaptive mass-based selection
determines a variable-cardinality KeepMask; and a survivor-conditioned decoder
uses that mask to update parameters and remaining knot positions together.
Control points remain the result of a standard least-squares spline solve.
Residual insertion and verified deletion are used only as a disclosed
comparison wrapper for sampled threshold experiments.

Repeated training, cross-source paired comparisons, and multi-tolerance tests
show that, when MSE and maximum pointwise squared error jointly define
feasibility, the complete pipeline attains the lowest overall mean
internal-knot count among the six evaluated implementations at the principal
setting. It also maintains a competitive joint-feasibility rate under stricter
bounds. Paired interventions further show that second-difference features,
learned parameters, and survivor-conditioned interaction materially affect
the raw fit, although source-level and mask-calibration results show no uniform
dominance. The evidence supports learned candidate selection as a useful
initializer for constrained numerical simplification, not as a replacement
for verification or a proof of minimality.
